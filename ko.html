샴페인을 좋아하는 사람이 되려고 계획한 건 아니었다. 의미 있는 일들이 다 그렇듯 — 서서히, 그러다 한꺼번에. 진짜 Grower Champagne이 처음 내 손에 들어온 순간, 무언가가 맞아 떨어졌다. 기포가 아니었다. 그 뒤에 있는 이야기였다.

지금은 Crystal Wine Group을 지원하고 있다 — 위대한 와인에는 위대한 사람이 필요하다는 믿음 위에 세워진 커뮤니티다. 소싱이든, 소개든, 단순한 공유든 — 목표는 같다: 맞는 병을 맞는 테이블로.

Les Clefs de Cristal Yangjae에서는 샴페인에 특별한 날이 필요하지 않은 공간을 만들었다. 있는 그대로 와서, 흥미로운 것을 열고, 와인이 말하게 두면 된다.

Bau Vineyard에서는 상상하지 못했던 일을 하고 있다 — 한국 땅에서 Chardonnay를 직접 기르는 것. 포도나무는 어리지만, 야망은 그렇지 않다. 매 시즌이 인내와 테루아에 대해 새로운 것을 가르친다.

Élise Bougy

Le Mont-Chainqueux — Premier Cru

Champagne · Les Mesneux PC · PN + Meunier · Vines 1965 · ~60yr old vines · ~10,000 bottles/year · Organic/biodynamic

포도밭을 '깨끗하게' 보여주기 위해, 셀러에서 최대한 덜 만지는 샴페인.

Élise Bougy는 2016년 가족의 3ha 포도밭을 이어받아 유기농·바이오다이내믹 전환을 시작했다. 연간 약 1만 병의 마이크로 도멘. Les Mesneux Premier Cru의 Le Mont-Chainqueux 파셀은 조부모가 1965년 심었다. Pinot Noir와 Meunier가 공동 식재, 수령 약 60년. 남동향. 아래쪽은 silt+chalk, 위쪽은 clay+sand — 이 토양의 대비가 와인의 구조를 만든다.

잔에서는 라즈베리, 붉은 사과, 체리가 먼저 오고 시간이 지나면 생아몬드, 브리오슈, 시나몬, 생강이 따라온다. 마지막은 철분을 연상시키는 미네랄, 소금기, chalk 피니시. 와인메이킹은 최소한의 개입 — 포도밭이 마지막 발언권을 갖는다. 그것이 이 와인의 전부다.

Gaspard Brochet

333.f Extra Brut & Le Lion Tome VII — Side by Side

Champagne · Écueil · b. 1995 · 3.2ha · Biodynamic from 2022 · Both 100% Pinot Noir · Two completely different characters

한쪽은 감칠맛, 다른 한쪽은 리덕션. 같은 Pinot Noir, 전혀 다른 두 얼굴.

Gaspard Brochet는 1995년생, 그래픽 디자이너 출신이다. 2017년 와인 생산 시작, 2019년 3.2ha 전체 이어받음. 2022 빈티지부터 전 포도밭 바이오다이내믹. 333.f는 2021년 수확량 감소로 기존 공식(3품종·3마을·3빈티지)을 따르지 못한 예외 — 100% Pinot Noir, 2022+2020 리저브, dosage 1g/L. 잔에서는 짭짤하고 고소한 감칠맛이 두드러진다.

Le Lion Tome VII는 Villers-aux-Nœuds PC의 단일 파셀 Le Mont Benoît. 2023 수확, 2026년 1월 디고르주망으로 매우 어린 병. 첫 향부터 강한 리덕션 — 성냥, 플린트, 연기. Gaspard는 실제로 배럴을 완전히 채우지 않을 만큼 산소를 적극 허용하는 생산자다. 이 리덕션은 스타일이 아니라 bottle state — 시간을 주면 과실이 열린다. 마케팅적으로 과소비되는 이름이지만, 와인은 상당히 재미있게 만든다.

Taittinger

Comtes de Champagne Blanc de Blancs 2008

Champagne · 100% Chardonnay · Avize + Cramant + Le Mesnil GC · Circling 100 · WK 98 · WS 95 · First released 1952

새벽 2시. 무제한. 점수가 맞다. 세팅이 더 좋았다.

Comtes de Champagne는 1952년 첫 출시 Taittinger 프레스티지 큐베. Côte des Blancs의 세 Grand Cru — Avize, Cramant, Le Mesnil-sur-Oger — 의 Chardonnay만으로. 2008 빈티지: 차가운 여름과 9월 후반의 일조량이 만든 이례적인 해 — 높은 당도와 높은 산도가 동시에 달성됐다. Circling 100, WK 98, WS 95.

잔에서 시트러스 오일, 분필, 잘 익은 복숭아와 사과. 로스팅된 아몬드와 꿀에 절인 생강이 따라온다. 풍부한 질감, 뛰어난 산미 밸런스, 강한 미네랄. 피니시는 토스티하고 길다. 강렬하지만 투명한 와인 — 풍미가 명확하고 직접적이다.

Dom Pérignon

Rosé 2008 — Lady Gaga Limited Edition

Champagne · PN 59% + CH 41% (red wine 10%) · 10+ years lees · Disgorged June 2019 · Market release 2021

Dom Pérignon Rosé의 가장 이례적인 콜라보레이션.

2008 빈티지와 Lady Gaga의 만남. DP Rosé에서 레드 와인 비율 10%는 최근 릴리즈 중 최저. 15년 이상 lees 숙성, 2019년 디고르주망, 2021년 출시.

낮은 레드 와인 비율에도 rosé의 정체성은 명확하다 — 섬세한 과실, 복잡한 구조, 오랜 숙성이 만드는 깊이. 2008 빈티지의 구조감이 섬세한 rosé의 성격과 만나는 지점이 흥미롭다.

Jérôme Prévost — La Closerie

Grand Cru Extra Brut

Champagne · 2022: CH 50% (Le Mesnil GC) + PN 50% (Puisieulx GC) · 2021: 100% CH Le Mesnil GC · Limited edition · First released 2019

Jérôme Prévost의 유일한 Grand Cru 큐베.

Prévost는 Gueux의 Meunier 전문 생산자로 알려져 있다. La Closerie GC는 2019년 첫 출시 리미티드 에디션 — Chardonnay와 Pinot Noir의 Grand Cru 포도를 사용하며 외부 포도를 구매한다. 2022: CH 50% Le Mesnil + PN 50% Puisieulx. 2021: 100% CH Le Mesnil.

희소하고, 접근하기 어렵고, 그래서 더 인상적이다. Prévost의 섬세한 감각이 Grand Cru 원료와 만날 때 어떤 결과가 나오는지 궁금했다면 — 이것이 그 답이다.

Krug

Vintage Brut 2006

Champagne · PN 45% + CH 35% + Meunier 20% · Est. 1843 · Aged 10+ years before release · "Capricious and wayward year"

Krug가 '변덕스럽고 까다롭다'고 표현한 2006 빈티지.

Krug는 매년 빈티지 와인을 만들지 않는다. 2006년은 만들었다 — 이유가 있다. PN 45% / CH 35% / Meunier 20%. 비교적 더운 해였으나 다양한 포도밭에서의 선별 수확이 복잡성을 만든다.

Krug 측은 장기 숙성을 권장한다. 지금도 충분히 흥미롭지만, 더 기다릴 이유가 있는 빈티지다. '변덕스럽다'는 표현은 와인에게도, 이 해를 경작한 사람들에게도 공평한 설명이다.

Cédric Bouchard — Roses de Jeanne

VV Côte de Val Vilaine 2020 — Blanc de Noirs

Champagne · Val Vilaine (lieu-dit) · 1.45ha · 100% Pinot Noir · No oak · 4 atm · ~300–500 cases · Disgorged 2022

기포가 적다. 그래서 다른 방식으로 말한다.

Aube의 극소량 parcellaire 생산자 Cédric Bouchard. VV Côte de Val Vilaine은 약 4기압 — 일반 샴페인의 6기압보다 현저히 낮다. 오크 미사용, 연간 약 300~500 케이스.

기포가 적으면 질감이 다르다 — 와인이 앞에 나온다. Bouchard가 원하는 것이 바로 그것이다. VV = Val Vilaine, R20 = 2020 수확. 4기압의 샴페인이 일반 샴페인과 얼마나 다르게 느껴지는지 이 와인 앞에서 알게 된다.

Louis Roederer
Cristal Rosé 2008

Champagne · Reims · Montagne de Reims

빈티지가 있고 세대가 있다. 2008은 후자다 — 구조적으로 정밀하고 에너지로 가득 차 있어서 샴페인이 무엇이 될 수 있는지를 계속해서 재정의하는 해. 그 수확에서 나온 모든 위대한 와인들 가운데, Cristal Rosé는 독보적인 위치를 차지한다: Louis Roederer가 단일 도멘과 나눠온 한 세기의 대화 가운데 가장 친밀한 표현.

Cristal은 1876년부터 생산되어 왔다 — 원래 알렉산더 2세 황제의 요청으로, 그는 와인이 변조되지 않았다는 것을 확인할 수 있도록 투명한 크리스탈 병을 원했다. Rosé는 더욱 희귀하다: Cumières와 Aÿ의 도멘 직영 포도밭에서 Pinot Noir를 saignée 방식으로 채혈하고 Chardonnay를 블렌딩해 길이를 더했다. 출시 전 10년 숙성. 시장을 위해 만든 와인이 아니다. 셀러를 위해 만든 와인이다.

2008은 기다림이 옳았다는 것을 알면서 인내심 있게 기다려온 것의 침착함으로 잔에 도달한다. 노즈는 정밀하고 층위가 있다 — 야생 딸기, 블러드 오렌지, 말린 장미 꽃잎, 화이트 페퍼, 그리고 그 아래에 모든 것을 초점으로 끌어당기는 미네랄 백악. 팔레트에서 산도는 계시처럼 온다: 공격적이지 않고 구조적이며, 액자가 그림을 잡아주듯 과실을 부유 상태로 유지한다.

Le Privé

French Dining in Gangnam, and the Touch of Chef Kihak Lim — Le Privé at The Privy House

Among the many things that stay with you after a visit to The Privy House, Le Privé — the French dining restaurant on the 37th floor — is the one that lingers longest. The view is extraordinary, the atmosphere composed. But what the meal itself makes clear is that this is not simply a beautiful room with a restaurant inside it.

What made it immediately interesting was the involvement of Chef Kihak Lim as consultant. The menu follows the classical architecture of French dining, but without the weight or the intimidation that can come with it. Familiar French cooking rendered in a present-day register — the fundamentals of sauce and technique intact, but worn lightly. You sense, eating here, that the food has its own centre of gravity.

Soupe à l'Oignon. Onion soup is one of those dishes whose quality is impossible to hide — it is too familiar, and its grammar too well known. Long-cooked onion sweetness, deep umami, the nutty warmth of Emmental and Gruyère. The concentration is present without heaviness. A bowl that sets the direction of a meal without announcing itself.

Poisson Meunière. Fish gently cooked and set in a sauce built on vermouth, fish stock and butter. The argument for meunière, as a technique, is ultimately about how elegantly the butter is handled — whether it amplifies the fish or overwhelms it. Here the butter is distinct and present, and the fish remains the subject.

Magret de Canard. The surprise of the evening was the duck — and the unexpected highlight was what accompanied it: French fries cooked in duck fat. Something so simple, done in duck fat, becomes something else entirely. The surface is crisp, the potato flavour clean, and behind it a low register of duck that stays. It was as memorable as the main itself. Duck as a protein rewards proper cooking with a depth that pulls you toward Burgundy Pinot Noir, an aged Bordeaux, or a more structured Champagne.

French dining's pleasure lies here: you need not reach for rare or complex ingredients. What changes the depth of a dish is how the butter, stock, sauce and fat are handled. Le Privé's menu — soupe à l'oignon, salade lyonnaise, beef tartare, duck croquette, meunière, bouillabaisse, braised pork, magret, steak — is familiar from a hundred French bistros and brasseries. What is not familiar is the precision with which it has been assembled for Seoul, and for now.

If The Privy House draws you with its view and its privacy, Le Privé is what makes you stay. Good dining is not completed by interior design alone — it requires people, food, and wine, held together by a chef's experience and instinct. That was the reason to see The Privy House differently this time. Not a room with a view, but a place to eat real French food with a good bottle. With Chef Kihak Lim's hand on Le Privé, it is worth coming back slowly, and with wine.

Onjium

Tradition is not something old. It is something still alive — A summer lunch at Michelin-starred Onjium

On the 8th of July, I visited Onjium, a Michelin one-star restaurant situated to the west of Gyeongbokgung Palace. To call it simply a Korean fine dining restaurant would be to leave too much unsaid. Onjium is first a traditional culture research institute, founded in 2013, which investigates Korean ways of dressing, eating and living — ui, sik, ju — and attempts to re-articulate them in a contemporary language. The restaurant is where the research of the food atelier, mat-gongbang, is realised as actual dishes. True to its name — which carries the sense of building something properly and wholly — every plate is accompanied by inquiry and interpretation.

The kitchen is led by Head Chef Eunhee Cho and Executive Chef Seongbae Park. Chef Cho is a certified inheritor of Joseon royal court cuisine, having studied at the Royal Cuisine Research Institute before serving as education team leader and adjunct university professor; she was among the founding members of Onjium in 2013, and in 2026 was named Asia's Best Female Chef. Chef Park bridges the atelier's research into the lived experience of a restaurant meal. Together, rather than restoring historical recipes wholesale, they recompose them in relation to today's ingredients and sensibility.

The season is legible in Onjium's courses. A summer progression might begin with grains and vegetables, gather weight through fish and seafood, and settle into meat and rice before closing with fruit and desserts that reimagine traditional beverages. The forms are recognisable — porridge, dumpling, jeon, jjim, noodle, banchan — but the concentration, temperature and texture of each dish have been quietly and precisely calibrated.

Seasonal markers such as corn, local fish, crustaceans, white cucumber and cornelian cherry appear when summer calls for them. Because Onjium's menu shifts continuously with research themes and ingredient availability, the more rewarding way to approach the meal is not to track individual dishes but to observe how a single season moves through the arc of an entire course.

The room speaks as clearly as the food. Exposed concrete, metal and warm timber come together in a space that is modern without coldness. White tables, composed tableware and ceramics bearing the evidence of a craftsperson's hand rest in considered negative space. At the counter facing the open kitchen, the choreography of the kitchen is visible and close.

Through wide windows, Gyeongbokgung Palace, the trees of Seochon and the ridge of Bugaksan extend outward. In the middle of the day, natural light travels deep into the room and changes the quality of glaze and surface on every piece of pottery. To look down at a palace while eating food that departs from centuries-old techniques: it is here that Onjium's space and its cooking make their most persuasive argument together.

Onjium's food neither defamiliarises tradition into strangeness nor fixes it behind glass like a museum object. It honours old records and techniques while drawing them quietly forward to the point where we might find them delicious, today. What I encountered at Onjium in July was less a past version of Korean food than Korean food as it continues to be made — across a long time, and still now.

Sutimun

Charcoal Heat, Palace Time — Chef Yunhaeng Heo's Sutimun and the New Language of Korean Dining

A restaurant's transformation is rarely announced by a change in a few dishes. What truly signals change is the decision about what to place at the centre — and in which direction to extend the restaurant's existing identity. The shift perceptible at Sutimun since Chef Yunhaeng Heo took over the kitchen in February begins exactly there.

Sutimun was originally built around a sequence dining concept centred on charcoal and moon — a progression through vegetables, seafood, poultry and meat, each course composed like a discrete scene, bound together by charcoal cookery, craft objects and spatial design into a single coherent narrative. The visible hearth and the rhythm of service around it were unmistakably Sutimun's own.

Under Chef Heo, Sutimun has not abandoned that identity. Instead it is deepening it — moving in a distinctly more Korean direction. The current episode is titled 고궁진연 古宮珍宴 — Royal Feast, the Resonance of Time: the culinary culture of the old palace, the work of contemporary Korean craft artists, and the produce of regional growers, brought together over charcoal and rendered as a modern course.

Not reproduction, but translation. Court cuisine is often imagined as elaborate presentation and formal display. But its essence lies elsewhere — in the balanced use of meat, vegetable and seafood; in the ordered deployment of diverse techniques (steaming, grilling, hot pot, pan-fried, blanched, raw) within a single meal; in the accumulated craft of dedicated cooks, transmitted across generations. Chef Heo's approach is less the restoration of historical dishes than the translation of court cuisine's underlying order, harmony and precision into the language of today.

At Sutimun, charcoal is not merely a concept. It sears a surface quickly, locking in texture; it concentrates the moisture and sweetness within an ingredient; it adds a low, persistent register of aroma that steaming or simmering cannot. But charcoal also carries a risk — if the smoke is too assertive, it overwhelms delicate ingredients and pulls every dish toward the same flavour. The grammar of court cuisine, which prizes restraint and balance, could easily clash with an emphatic char.

What impressed me most was precisely that Sutimun does not make charcoal the protagonist. In the dishes that worked best, the char was not the first thing you registered — it was the last: a brief trailing note that arrived after the sweetness and umami of the ingredient had already been drawn out. This is cookery that uses fire not to demonstrate fire, but to clarify what the ingredient already is.

Orchestrating the refinement of court cuisine and the raw energy of charcoal within a single course is not straightforward. Rather than forcing the two together, Sutimun positions charcoal as a punctuation mark inside the ordered flow that court cuisine provides. That restraint is the most persuasive evidence of what has changed under Chef Heo.

Sutimun's transformation is interesting because it has not erased the past in the name of novelty. Placing the time of the palace on the heat of charcoal; finding, in the quick rhythms of Yeouido, the long breath that Korean food has always carried. That is the most meaningful thing Chef Yunhaeng Heo's Sutimun is showing us now.

Sosuheon

The Art of Balance — Chef Kyungjae Park's Gastronomy

What I felt most acutely at Sosuheon — the recently crowned Michelin two-star — was the philosophy that Chef Kyungjae Park has spent a lifetime building. Over more than thirty years devoted entirely to sushi, through the Shilla Hotel and Kojima before opening his own space, he led Korea's first Michelin two-star sushi at Kojima and received the Michelin Mentor Chef Award in 2023. Each seasonal ingredient was read with precision — umami present but never excessive, salinity calibrated to awaken the palate without overwhelming it, acidity functioning like negative space between courses. Nothing jutted forward; everything supported everything else. What struck me was not the ability to add flavour, but the ability to edit it.

Moving into the nigiri, that delicacy became even more pronounced. The neta was captured at its precise peak. The shari — warm, correctly salted, dissolving with a gentle give — existed not to assert itself but to make the fish more vivid. The technique Chef Park has refined over decades manifests not as spectacle but as a balance so natural the diner barely registers it. Michelin describes Sosuheon as a place where "the refined touch of a master who quietly focuses on forming each nigiri" defines the experience — and sitting before the counter, that description felt exactly right.

One particularly memorable moment was the lobster with truffle. It is a combination where truffle can easily colonise every other flavour, but here it did not. The sweetness and yielding texture of the lobster remained at the centre; the truffle was given the role of layering fragrance, nothing more. Luxurious ingredients were deployed, yet the ingredient itself remained the subject. What lingered longest after the meal was the word restraint. Many restaurants reach for intensity to produce surprise. Sosuheon never loses its balance. In the end, what was most delicious at Sosuheon was not any single dish. It was the balance that Chef Kyungjae Park never once let go of — from the first bite to the last.

Restaurant Report · July 22, 2026

Restaurant SAN

From the World's Kitchens, Back to the Taste of Korea

On July 22nd, I sat down to experience the summer menu at Restaurant SAN. At the heart of this Michelin-recognised destination is Chef Seunghyun Cho — a Korean-born cook who spent years refining his culinary language in the world's most demanding kitchens before returning home.

His résumé reads like a pilgrimage through fine dining's inner sanctum: The French Laundry in Napa Valley, Troisgros in Roanne, Piazza Duomo in Italy, and Geranium in Copenhagen. He then made his way to San Francisco, joining Chef Corey Lee at the three-Michelin-starred Benu, where he spent roughly eight years as Chef de Cuisine.

Benu is a restaurant that interprets the flavours of Korea, China, and East Asia through the precision of Western fine dining. Working there, Chef Cho didn't simply acquire technique — he learned how to translate the taste of one culture into the language of another. That foundation of classical French rigour and deep East Asian sensibility is what underpins everything SAN stands for today.

He returned to Korea and opened his first independent restaurant in 2024. SAN is not a showcase for what he learned abroad — it is closer to a personal reckoning: after years away, a careful re-examination of Korean flavour through his own eyes and hands.

Two currents run through the food simultaneously. The first is the precision absorbed from world-class kitchens — the calibration of sauce density and temperature, the contrast of textures, the deliberate arc of flavour within a single plate. The second is the Korea lodged in his memory: jeotgal, dongchimi, milmyeon, dwaeji gukbap. Both are present, and neither overwhelms the other.

The Michelin Guide describes SAN as a restaurant that pairs the intensity of seasonal ingredients with complex sauces while maintaining restrained balance. It singles out the abalone jang, tofu flower, and pork gukbap as signature expressions of Chef Cho's imagination and Korean interpretation. That direction was unmistakable in the summer course.

The meal opens with the coolness and bright acidity of summer produce, then builds gradually — through the umami of seafood, the depth of fermentation, and the warmth of meat and fire — without ever becoming heavy. The sauces are layered but never mask the ingredient. The technique is evident but never intrudes. You sense the years in America and Europe, but every dish ultimately resolves toward Korea.

The space suits the name perfectly. Tucked underground, the restaurant is insulated from the city above. Inside, the architecture echoes forests and ridgelines — tree-ring textures, restrained lighting, dark tones — creating a quiet that allows the food and wine to hold the room.

In 2026, SAN received the Asia's 50 Best Restaurants One To Watch Award — recognition for a restaurant still early in its journey, yet already carrying the weight of everything Chef Cho accumulated across the world's great kitchens.

A chef who spent years in the world's finest kitchens, finally returning to the taste he was always moving toward. The summer at Restaurant SAN felt like both a record of the distance he has travelled and a clear signal of where he intends to go.
