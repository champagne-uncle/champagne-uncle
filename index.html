Domaine Vincey

Chemin de Châlons · Grand Cru Blanc de Blancs 2021

Le Mesnil-sur-Oger · Côte des Blancs · Champagne

Le Mesnil appears in every serious conversation about Chardonnay in Champagne. Sharp acidity, chalky minerality, ageing potential measured in decades — and within this village, a new generation is interpreting the terroir on its own terms.

Domaine Vincey was founded in 2014 by Quentin Vincey and Marine Zabarino. They farm Grand Cru vineyards in Le Mesnil-sur-Oger and Oger organically and biodynamically, and since 2020 have effectively eliminated SO₂ from the winemaking process. Native yeast fermentation and extended lees ageing are the tools; the goal is to bring the character of each vineyard to the glass with as little interference as possible. All wines are defined by their lieu-dit rather than a house style. Chemin de Châlons is where that philosophy is stated most clearly.

The vineyard sits on a southeast-facing slope in Le Mesnil Grand Cru. The vines were planted in 1968 by selection massale. Below a thin layer of clay lies the pure chalk that defines Le Mesnil — soil that gives Chardonnay tension rather than generosity, minerality rather than fruit.

On the nose, the Chardonnay is pure and transparent: lemon zest, yuzu, green apple, white peach, pear. With time, white blossom, lemon balm, roasted hazelnut, and a faint smokiness arrive. The oak does not assert itself — it softens and connects the texture without drawing attention.

On the palate, Le Mesnil's identity becomes more pronounced. The first impression is upright and linear — ripe citrus and white fruit, with chalk and limestone minerality holding the centre. Creamy texture from lees ageing wraps around that tension. Zero dosage, but nothing harsh: a fine saltiness and delicate acidity build the balance, and the finish extends with lemon-peel bitterness and the sensation of wet stone.

Light, pure, mineral and acid-driven. A wine to drink with food rather than to cellar further.

"Zero dosage, but nothing harsh — a fine saltiness and the sensation of wet stone carry the finish."

— Champagne Uncle

Champagne Tarlant

Cuvée Louis · Brut Nature

Base 2004–2005 · Dégorgement 06.06.2022 · Œuilly, Vallée de la Marne

Tarlant is a family grower based in Œuilly in the Vallée de la Marne, with roots in viticulture stretching back to the seventeenth century. Today the domaine is led by Benoît and Mélanie Tarlant. Their philosophy is not to make Champagne as a fresh, effervescent wine — it is to reveal the character of the vineyard and the texture that comes from extended ageing, with dosage minimised or eliminated entirely so the wine's inherent structure speaks for itself.

Cuvée Louis is the cuvée that best represents the domaine's history and style. The name honours the ancestor Louis Tarlant, who first bottled wine from the family's own vineyards. The blend is 50% Pinot Noir and 50% Chardonnay, centred on the old vines of Les Crayons in Œuilly. Fermented in oak with native yeast, no malolactic fermentation. Bottled in 2006, then aged on lees for approximately sixteen years before disgorgement on 6 June 2022. Zero dosage.

On the nose, ripe yellow apple, pear, apricot, orange peel and lemon curd open first. As temperature rises, fig, honeycomb, candied ginger, roasted almond and hazelnut, brioche, butter pastry and faint vanilla layer in behind.

On the palate, the texture is wide and soft to begin. The weight of Pinot Noir fruit and the acidity of Chardonnay balance each other, and the long lees contact fills the mid-palate with savoury, toasted depth. But the core of this wine is in the back half. Lemon-peel bitterness, oyster shell and chalk minerality, and a fine saltiness tighten the finish and restore structure after all that richness.

A beautiful wine — high-density texture and generosity, earned through time.

"Sixteen years on lees, zero dosage — and the finish tightens rather than fades. Chalk, oyster shell, salt."

— Champagne Uncle

Ulysse Collin

Le Jardin d'Ulysse — Base 2018 · 72 Months on Lees

Champagne · Congy, Coteaux du Petit Morin · CH + PN + Meunier · 1.55ha · Clay-silex over limestone · N & S-facing slopes · 15% 2017 CH reserve · Natural yeast · Oak fermentation · MLF · Dosage to balance · First base vintage: 2015

Expressing a single vineyard does not require a single variety. Le Jardin d'Ulysse is the proof.

While the other cuvées of Ulysse Collin are anchored to single varieties — Les Maillons to Pinot Noir, Les Enfers and Les Pierrières to Chardonnay — Le Jardin d'Ulysse begins with a vineyard where Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, and Meunier grow together. The name means "Ulysse's Garden." It is the parcel immediately beside the house and winery.

Olivier Collin recovered the family vineyards from lease in 2003 and began making wine under his own label in 2004. The influence of Burgundy on his approach is clear in the commitment to showing each parcel as a distinct wine — the vineyard, not the house style, as the subject. The first base vintage for Le Jardin was 2015.

The Vineyard. 1.55 hectares in Congy, known on maps as "Le Village" and locally as "Le Clos." Clay and silex topsoil over limestone subsoil. Both north- and south-facing slopes. In a warm year like 2018, the dominant north-facing aspect was an asset: enough ripeness from a generous harvest, while the exposure helped retain freshness and tension that a purely south-facing site might have sacrificed.

Base 2018 — what the label means. "Base 2018" does not mean the wine is made entirely from 2018 grapes. As a non-vintage Champagne, it includes 15% reserve wine — specifically 2017 Chardonnay. Olivier has said that his aim is not to reproduce a vintage but to find the best expression of the vineyard. The reserve component broadens what a single year alone could show.

2018 was also a year of technical refinement. In his harvest notes, Olivier described the quality of the fruit as unusually concentrated and pure, and noted that a new press introduced that year had improved the precision of juice extraction. The precision Olivier talks about does not begin with time on lees — it begins at the press.

Winemaking. Natural yeast fermentation in oak (primarily used Burgundy barrels, with larger wood vessels also used for ageing). Malolactic fermentation carried out. No fining, no filtration. Dosage is not minimised as a principle — it is left at whatever the wine needs for balance and ageing. 72 months on lees before disgorgement.

Nose. Orange oil, pear, and mirabelle plum at the centre. Freshly baked bread and hazelnut layered across. This is not a wine of pure mineral tension and citrus extension — it has fruit density and brioche-and-nut richness alongside.

Palate. Full-bodied and broad in texture, but structured underneath. Rich mid-palate fruit without the acidity loosening. A chalky mineral quality and a pleasant salinity arrive at the finish. 72 months on lees does not mean the wine has peaked — lees ageing and post-disgorgement bottle development are different processes, and this wine still has things to say. The contrast between fruit density and acidity is worth watching in a well-kept bottle.

Worth collecting, if the opportunity comes.

"A vineyard beside the house, three varieties, 72 months. Le Jardin d'Ulysse is not a statement about method. It is a statement about a place."

— Champagne Uncle

Louis Roederer

Cristal Rosé 2014

Champagne · Aÿ, Avize, Le Mesnil-sur-Oger Grand Cru · PN 55% + CH 45% · 4 Parcels · Infusion method · Oak 19% · Zero MLF · Dosage 8g/l · Harvest 11–21 Sept 2014

Four parcels. The caprice of 2014. And a precision that outlasted both.

Cristal Rosé is, for me, among the finest rosé Champagnes produced anywhere. The regular Cristal is outstanding — but Cristal Rosé occupies a different world entirely, defined by its scarcity, its four specific parcels, and an unusual method of production that sets it apart from any conventional rosé assemblage.

It was first made in 1974 by Jean-Claude Rouzaud. The defining characteristic is the vineyards: just four parcels, all Grand Cru, spread across Aÿ, Avize, and Le Mesnil-sur-Oger. Ripe, generous Pinot Noir from Aÿ meets the chalk-driven tension and acidity of Côte des Blancs Chardonnay. The architecture of the wine is built from that contrast.

The Infusion Method. Rather than adding still red wine at assemblage — the standard approach — Roederer uses a technique they call Infusion. Chardonnay juice is introduced directly into the Pinot Noir maceration and the two ferment together. Colour, flavour, and texture are integrated from the very beginning, not blended in afterward. The result is a rosé that does not separate into its components on the palate.

2014 — A Difficult Year Resolved. 2014 was not an easy vintage. A warm and dry spring, a heatwave in June, then a cool and wet summer — rescued, finally, by a dry and clear September. What made the difference was the chalk subsoil: its drainage expelled the excess moisture of summer and prevented the vines from overgrowing. The result was full ripeness combined with high acidity — exactly what Cristal Rosé demands. Harvest ran from 11 to 21 September. The blend: PN 55% / CH 45%, oak-aged at 19%, zero malolactic fermentation, dosage 8 g/l. No MLF is not a statement of austerity — it is a choice to preserve the acidity that 2014 provided naturally.

On the Nose. Red currant, wild raspberry, strawberry, cassis at the opening. Then blood orange, sweet spice, a light smokiness and a reductive hint of toasted grain underneath. Complex from the start, and not in a hurry to reveal itself.

On the Palate. Silky texture and rich red fruit arrive first. Then, progressively, the acidity rises — mineral, saline, with iodine undertones that strengthen toward the finish. What is most impressive about 2014 is precisely this: it is not a wine of richness. It is a wine of refined acidity. The density is there, but it never softens into ease. The scores reflect it — Galloni (Vinous) 98, Suckling 98, Kelley (WA) 97, Dunnuck 97.

On international retail markets, the 2014 has been trading at approximately €600 per bottle. The scarcity is real — the parcels are narrower than standard Cristal, and production only happens in vintages that merit it.

Twenty-plus years of ageing potential. If you are opening one now, allow at least three hours of breathing. This is a wine that needs time to show what it is.

"The appeal of Cristal Rosé 2014 is not its rarity or its price. It is that 2014 — a year that could have produced something merely pretty — produced something precise."

— Champagne Uncle

Henri Giraud

PR 90–21 — Perpetual Reserve Extra Brut

Champagne · Aÿ Grand Cru · 80% Pinot Noir + 20% Chardonnay · Perpetual Reserve since 1990 · Dosage 5g/l · ~20,000 bottles

Not blending vintages — brewing time itself.

At first glance, Henri Giraud PR 90–21 looks like a champagne blending the 1990 and 2021 vintages. But that's not the point. PR stands for Perpetual Reserve — a réserve perpétuelle that draws from a living, evolving reserve begun in 1990, replenished annually, never fully emptied. Every year, a portion is drawn out and replaced with wine from the new harvest, so the past never disappears — it carries forward into the next generation.

And PR 90–21 is not a conventional Non-Vintage Champagne with a base wine plus reserve additions. One hundred percent of the finished wine is perpetual reserve. The composition: 80% Pinot Noir and 20% Chardonnay, centred on a reserve running from 1990 to 2021, with approximately three years of lees ageing after bottling. La Revue du Vin de France records the dosage at 5 g/l (Extra Brut) and production at approximately 20,000 bottles.

How the Reserve Works. Henri Giraud's approach is not to empty the reserve tank fully each year, but to draw off a portion and replenish it with new wine — so that the older wines persist into the next cycle, and the next. The perpetual reserve has been building since 1990, replenished at approximately 20% per year. What is unusual is the vessel: not stainless steel, but metal-lined concrete tanks, stored in cellars beneath the Marne river, maintaining a stable natural temperature of around 11°C year-round. Vibration and temperature fluctuation are minimised. This is not simply a matter of storage — it is an environment designed to slow oxidation and allow wines from different years to integrate slowly and completely over time.

What Three Years of Bottle Ageing Does. After final blending, PR 90–21 spends approximately three years ageing on lees in bottle. The wine entering bottle already carries decades of development from within the reserve tank — then undergoes a second, entirely different kind of ageing: secondary fermentation and autolysis. The compounds released as yeast cells break down make the texture progressively creamier, and contribute notes of brioche, bread crust, and pastry. The complexity of PR does not come from one type of ageing. It comes from three overlapping processes: micro-oxidation in oak, long development in the perpetual reserve, and reductive ageing on lees in bottle. Different directions, running simultaneously. That is why the wine resists a single description.

"PR 90–21 is not a wine made from decisions about which years to blend. It is a wine made from a decision about how time should be kept — and what it means to pour something that has been accumulating since 1990."

— Champagne Uncle

Élise Bougy

Le Mont-Chainqueux — Premier Cru

Champagne · Les Mesneux PC · PN + Meunier · Vines 1965 · ~60yr old vines · ~10,000 bottles/year · Organic/biodynamic

The fruit is generous. But what stays is the texture — dry chalk and salt at the finish, the terroir pulling everything back toward the ground it came from.

Élise Bougy studied wine business and viticulture in Reims, Dijon, and Avize, then worked in wine distribution and sales before founding her own agency, Les Bouchons d'Élise, in 2014. In 2016 she inherited around 3 hectares of family vineyard and began converting to organic and biodynamic farming. Her first harvest under her own label was 2018. The domaine is tiny — approximately 2.8 to 3 hectares, around 10,000 bottles per year. Two-thirds of the vineyard is in Les Mesneux Premier Cru on the Montagne de Reims, the rest in Le Mesnil-sur-Oger Grand Cru on the Côte des Blancs. That contrast — Pinot Noir and Meunier from Mesneux, Chardonnay from Le Mesnil — defines the range.

Le Mont-Chainqueux. A family parcel in Les Mesneux Premier Cru, planted by Élise's grandparents in 1965. Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier co-planted in the same vineyard. Vines now approaching sixty years old. South-east facing. Within the parcel, the soil changes: lower slopes are silt over chalk; upper slopes are clay and sand. This internal variation matters — the upper portion's sand and clay soften the red fruit and flesh, while the chalk and silt below pull the finish back toward dryness and mineral precision.

In the Glass. Raspberry, red apple, a touch of cherry — the red fruit leads clearly. Over time: raw almond, brioche, cinnamon, ginger. French tasting records note raspberry, brioche, almond, cinnamon, clove, and a consistently vinous texture. On the palate: the flesh of Pinot Noir and Meunier arrives first, soft and rounded. The bubbles are subtle rather than assertive. The finish: a faint metallic iron mineral, salinity, chalk. The terroir reclaims the wine at the end. That contrast — generous fruit, restrained mineral conclusion — is what makes this wine interesting.

The winemaking philosophy is minimal intervention: the goal is to show the vineyard without processing it away in the cellar. Le Mont-Chainqueux is a good test of whether that approach works. It does.

"The fruit is wide and generous — Pinot Noir and Meunier from 60-year-old vines in Les Mesneux. But at the end, the chalk comes in and closes it. The vineyard gets the last word. That is the whole idea, and it works."

— Champagne Uncle

Gaspard Brochet

333.f Extra Brut & Le Lion Tome VII — Side by Side

Champagne · Écueil · b. 1995 · 3.2ha · Biodynamic from 2022 · Both 100% Pinot Noir · Two completely different characters

One leads with umami. The other leads with reduction. Same grape, same producer, same village — and two entirely different faces. That contrast is the point.

Gaspard Brochet was born in 1995. He studied graphic design — his labels are a direct expression of that background, and they are among the most visually distinctive in Champagne today. Around 2013 he returned to his family's village of Écueil. In 2017 he began producing under his own name from part of the family vineyard. In 2019 he took over the full 3.2ha domaine. The parcels — 14 of them — are spread across Écueil, Sacy, and Villers-aux-Nœuds, with some vines dating to the 1950s and 60s. Since the 2022 vintage, the entire domaine is farmed biodynamically. His father is Vincent Brochet, but Gaspard operates independently.

333.f — Left Bottle (Umami). The concept behind 333 is three grape varieties, three villages, three vintages. But the 2021 frost and harvest failure forced an exception. 333.f is 100% Pinot Noir — 2022 wine blended with 2020 reserve. Old vines averaging ~50 years from the three villages, on a soil that is approximately 80% sand, 10% clay, 10% chalk. Fermented and aged 11 months in 10hl vessels. 24 months bottle ageing after secondary fermentation. Dosage: 1g/l. What stands out in the glass is an unusual savoury quality — umami, saline, toasty, with apple and nuts behind it. Not a typical Pinot profile. Something more textural and food-like.

Le Lion Tome VII — Right Bottle (Reduction). Single parcel: the lieu-dit 'Le Mont Benoît' in Villers-aux-Nœuds Premier Cru. 100% Pinot Noir, vines planted 1985, silt over shallow chalk. 'Tome' is used as a publishing term — each vintage is a new volume of the same ongoing story. Tome VII is the 2023 harvest. Disgorged January 2026 — very recently at the time of tasting. The first impression is strong: struck match, flint, smoke, something roasted, and a fairly closed fruit underneath. Reduction, unmistakably.

On the Reduction. It would be wrong to conclude that Gaspard Brochet vinifies reductively. He does the opposite — he deliberately allows oxygen contact during base wine production, leaving barrels partially unfilled. The reduction in Tome VII is not a house style. It is a bottle state — the result of secondary fermentation, extended lees contact, and a disgorgement that happened only months before the glass. With time in the glass the fruit opens. The wine is simply young. Very young.

The comparison was genuinely interesting — same producer, same primary variety, same broad geography, two completely different results. The hype around the Brochet name occasionally outpaces the wine, but the wines themselves are serious and worth paying attention to.

"333.f asks you to eat. Le Lion asks you to wait. Both are Pinot Noir from Écueil. One has been there — the umami, the texture, the savouriness. The other is still becoming itself. Gaspard Brochet is 30 years old. So are the wines, in their own way."

— Champagne Uncle

Taittinger

Comtes de Champagne Blanc de Blancs 2008

Champagne · 100% Chardonnay · Avize + Cramant + Le Mesnil GC · Circling 100 · WK 98 · WS 95 · First released 1952

2am. Unlimited. A happy wine person's life. There are worse ways to confirm that 2008 is what everyone says it is.

Comtes de Champagne is Taittinger's prestige cuvée — first released in 1952, and one of the most recognised Blanc de Blancs in the world. It is built entirely from Chardonnay sourced from three Grand Cru villages in the Côte des Blancs: Avize, Cramant, and Le Mesnil-sur-Oger. Three different expressions of the same grape, and three different mineral signatures, assembled into a wine intended to express the full range of what Côte des Blancs Chardonnay can achieve.

The 2008 Vintage. Cold winter → late, frost-free budbreak → slow, dry, cool growing season that preserved acidity completely → September sunshine for the final ripening push. The result: a vintage with simultaneously high sugar and high acidity — unusual enough that it defined the standard against which 2008 Champagnes are still judged. Circling 100 points. William Kelley (Wine Advocate) 98 points. Wine Spectator 95 points. The numbers match the glass.

In the Glass. Citrus oil, chalk, ripe peach and apple — bright and clean on the nose. Roasted almond and honey-pickled ginger follow, adding warmth and complexity. On the palate: rich texture, excellent acid balance, strong mineral presence. The finish is toasty and long. The wine is intense but transparent — the flavours are clear and direct, not buried under oakiness or excessive yeast character. Creamy texture throughout. A wine of obvious quality that communicates it without effort.

For a 2008 at this level, the question of whether to drink or hold remains open. The vintage has decades of potential. This particular tasting — enjoyed at 2am with no limit on pours — suggests the answer is both.

"Circling 100. Parker 98. Spectator 95. Comtes 2008 at 2am, no limit. The scores are correct. The setting was better."

Dom Pérignon

Rosé 2008 — Lady Gaga Limited Edition

Champagne · PN 59% + CH 41% (red wine 10%) · 10+ years lees · Disgorged June 2019 · Market release 2021

The label is a collaboration with a pop icon. The wine inside is one of the most classically structured Dom Pérignon Rosés in recent memory.

Dom Pérignon Rosé 2008 was released in collaboration with Lady Gaga — an edition with a distinctive label design and a stated social message from the partnership. The collaboration is visible on the bottle. The wine inside is fully its own argument, unaffected by the packaging decision.

An Unusually Restrained Blend. The red wine component in Dom Pérignon Rosé varies significantly by vintage: 2004 reached 28%, 2009 dropped to 13%. The 2008 sits at just 10% — the lowest in recent memory. Total composition: Pinot Noir 59%, Chardonnay 41%, with 10% of that Pinot Noir as red wine. It is a deliberately restrained rosé — closer to a blanc de noirs tinted with red wine than to a deep, fruit-forward style. The result is a wine of precision rather than expressiveness.

The 2008 Vintage. One of Champagne's great contemporary vintages. A cold winter delayed budbreak, avoiding frost damage and ensuring abundant, healthy fruit. The growing season was dry and cool — which slowed phenolic maturity but allowed sugar and acidity to develop simultaneously. The paradox of 2008: at harvest, the grapes had both high acid and high sugar, which is rare. The result is a vintage defined by structure — taut, vivid, built for long ageing.

In the Glass. Ripe raspberry and violet on the nose — prominent florals. White pepper and cool herbs follow. On the palate: silky texture, full body, bold and assertive. The 2008 structure is immediately present — firm acidity, high energy, ripe red fruit. Mineral finish, long and sustained. This is not an easy, approachable rosé. It is powerful and precisely made, and it rewards attention.

Disgorged June 2019 after ten-plus years on lees. Released to market in 2021. The lees time is evident in the texture and depth.

"Only 10% red wine in the blend — the lowest in recent DP Rosé history. It is not trying to be immediately charming. It is trying to last. The structure of 2008 is doing what 2008 always does: making everything wait for it."

— Champagne Uncle

Bollinger

La Grande Année 2014 — Brut

Champagne · PN 61% + CH 39% · 19 GC & PC · Oak barrel 1st fermentation · 8+ years lees · Dosage 8g/l · Disgorged 2022

Disgorged 2022 — and it shows. The acidity has become electric, and the whole thing has pulled into focus. This is 2014 at its best.

Bollinger was founded in 1829 and remains one of the few grandes maisons still family-controlled. The house is defined by its commitment to Pinot Noir and its insistence on oak — Bollinger is one of the only Champagne houses to maintain its own cooperage, managing old oak barrels used for primary fermentation. All base wines undergo first fermentation in small barrels, allowing micro-oxidation to build complexity and ageing potential from the earliest stage of production.

La Grande Année. Bollinger's prestige vintage, produced only in exceptional years. All disgorgement and riddling by hand. Lees ageing at more than twice the legal minimum — eight years in this case. The 2014 is PN 61% from Aÿ and Verzenay, CH 39% from Chouilly and Oiry, across 19 classified vineyards in total. Dosage: 8g/l.

The 2014 Vintage. A dynamic, irregular year. Wet winter and early spring, followed by two unusually dry and sunny months — June was the sunniest in fifty years. But July and August were the coldest in half a century, slowing ripening significantly. September recovered: clear and warm, allowing full maturation. Bollinger started harvest five days after the official date, on 15 September, and completed everything in nine days — a tight window reflecting the concentrated ripening. The cold summer preserved acidity perfectly; the September sun delivered ripe fruit. The result: precision and freshness, rather than richness.

In the Glass. Vanilla and chalk up front, then apricot, candied orange, almond tart, brioche. Apple tart and flint follow. Medium-to-full body. The complexity is layered — yeast, fruit and oak balanced throughout. The finish is weighty but even. As the wine opens, the acidity sharpens noticeably, bringing real energy. Elegant and refined — the coolness of 2014 is unmistakable, and is the wine's greatest quality.

Cristal's history begins in 1876, when a clear crystal bottle was commissioned for Tsar Alexander II — lead-based dark glass was considered a security risk for a target of assassinations, and the transparency of crystal allowed the contents to be inspected. Grande Cuvée 173ème Édition — Brut

Champagne · Reims · Base 2017 · 13 vintages · 150 wines · 31% reserve · Disgorged early 2024

Good right now — but wait ten years and it will show you something else entirely.

To describe Krug Grande Cuvée simply as a "non-vintage Champagne" is to miss the point. Joseph Krug's ambition from 1843 was not to make great Champagne only in great years. It was to make the most complete Champagne possible every year — regardless of whether the vintage cooperated. Grande Cuvée does not erase a vintage; it compensates for what any single year lacks with the accumulated depth of many others.

2017 — Why Grande Cuvée Exists. The 173ème Édition is centred on 2017, a year that demonstrated the case for Krug's approach with particular clarity. The season began cold, then warmed quickly — vines woke early. Spring frost followed, damaging young shoots. May and June brought fine weather and good flowering, but heavy summer rain raised disease pressure sharply through July and August. Official records show approximately 20% of potential harvest lost. The late-season heat saved what remained. Harvest ran 25 August to 15 September. Red varieties suffered most from rot; extreme selection was unavoidable. Some parcels and wines did not pass Krug's standards and were set aside entirely.

150 Wines, 13 Vintages. The final blend draws on 150 individual wines from 13 different years — the youngest from 2017, the oldest reaching back to 2001. Reserve wines account for 31% of the final blend. The proportions: Pinot Noir 44%, Chardonnay 34%, Meunier 22%. Dosage: 4.5 g/l. Disgorged early 2024.

173 vs 172. Comparing the 173ème to its predecessor — base vintage 2016 — is the clearest way to frame the difference. The 172 shows stronger acidity and tension; the 173 is broader, more generous, more immediately approachable. 2016 was a tighter vintage structurally; 2017, for all its difficulties, produced fruit with more volume and expressiveness where the harvest survived.

In the Glass. The nose is led by pear, golden apple, lemon curd and nutmeg, followed by vanilla pastry cream, stone fruit pie and passionfruit. On the palate: medium body, an exceptionally fine and creamy mousse, high-tension acidity, and a long finish with a pleasant salinity running through it. The drinking window has just opened. If you value fruit expression above all, drink before 2030. If you are willing to wait, ten years will reveal a different face.

Marguet

Sapience 2017 — Brut Nature

Champagne · Ambonnay Grand Cru · 68% Meunier · 32% Pinot Noir · Zero dosage · ~6y 8m sur lattes

A year without Chardonnay — made from the two varieties that 2017 treated most harshly.

To describe Marguet simply as a "natural Champagne" producer is to leave too much unsaid. The domaine traces its roots to around 1875 and Émile Marguet — a figure who, according to the official Champagne producer directory, was already experimenting with grafting vines in the Côte des Blancs and Grande Montagne de Reims before phylloxera arrived in force in 1883. Today Benoît Marguet runs the estate from Ambonnay: 8 hectares in total, 7.30ha in Ambonnay and 0.70ha in Bouzy, with an average vine age of around forty years.

The Farming. Biodynamic viticulture, phytotherapy using medicinal plant infusions, and aromatherapy with essential oils. Since 2010, the soil has been worked by horse. The result is a wine that carries the texture and the particular umami of soil cultivated without mechanical compaction.

Sapience: A Collaboration. Sapience's first vintage was 2006 — a project Benoît co-created with winemaker Hervé Jestin, drawing on grapes from several biodynamic growers: David Léclapart's Chardonnay, Georges Laval's Meunier, Benoît Lahaye's Pinot Noir. The original template was roughly 50% Chardonnay, 25% Meunier, 25% Pinot Noir — a prestige cuvée built from collaborative sourcing.

2017: No Chardonnay. The 2017 is a complete departure. Chardonnay is absent. The blend is 68% Meunier from Georges Laval's vineyards in Cumières and Hautvillers, and 32% Pinot Noir from Benoît Marguet's Ambonnay. The two most vulnerable varieties in one of Champagne's most difficult recent vintages.

The Vintage. 2017 was genuinely hard. A dry winter gave way to early spring and early budburst. Then April frost arrived — approximately 22,000 hectares across Champagne were affected. May and June were warm and dry, which helped. But from late July, the weather shifted: rain and humidity rose sharply through August. Botrytis appeared; in some areas, sour rot followed. Pinot Noir and Meunier suffered far more than Chardonnay. INAO records Meunier as the most damaged variety of the year; strict selection in the vineyard was unavoidable. Choosing precisely these two varieties for Sapience 2017 was not a concession — it was a statement.

Vinification & Ageing. Bottled in June 2018. Aged on lees for approximately six years and eight months. Disgorged February 2025. Zero dosage — nothing added at disgorgement.

In the Glass. The nose opens on Meunier's characteristic intensity and umami depth: grapefruit and citrus, dried apple and pear, orange peel, dried herbs, a spicy edge, and prominent brioche. On the palate, strong acidity and concentrated fruit arrive together, followed by saline salinity and persistent umami. Fruit and acidity are still leading — this wine needs time. It is not yet at the centre of what it will become.

Pierre Paillard

Les Parcelles XX — Bouzy Grand Cru Extra Brut

Champagne · Bouzy · Montagne de Reims · 8th Generation · Est. 1768

Bouzy's sun ripens the Pinot Noir. The chalk below pulls it back into tension. This is the whole argument in one glass.

The first sip is generous. Ripe pear and apple, orange, red fruit — and behind them, toasted bread, nuts, a quiet spice. But as the wine moves toward the finish, the character shifts entirely. Acidity and salinity arrive; the dry, chalky texture takes over and lingers. The contrast between the two halves is the point. Pierre Paillard's Champagne is ultimately a question of how to express a warm Pinot Noir terroir without letting it become heavy — and Les Parcelles XX is where that question is posed most directly.

Eight Generations in Bouzy. The Paillard family has been in Bouzy since the early eighteenth century. The official record begins with Antoine Paillard's purchase of Bouzy vineyards in 1768. The current generation — brothers Antoine and Quentin — is the eighth. The modern house, Champagne Pierre Paillard, dates to 1946, when their grandfather Pierre left the cooperative after the Second World War, installed his own press, and began vinifying independently. Benoît Paillard followed; his two sons now run the domaine.

Since 2016. The brothers have sharpened their direction around four principles: conversion to organic farming, soil-respecting viticulture, lower yields in pursuit of full ripeness, and — above all — extended élevage in oak. Reduced. Riper. More patient. That is the summary.

Understanding Bouzy. To understand Pierre Paillard, you need to understand Bouzy. It sits on the south-facing slope of the Montagne de Reims, alongside Ambonnay — together they produce the most powerful and structured Pinot Noir in Champagne. The southern exposure matters: compared to north-facing Grand Cru villages like Verzenay or Verzy, fruit here ripens more completely. Ripe apple, cherry, red fruit, plum, spice, and a broad texture are the signatures. Bouzy tends to be rounder than Ambonnay, more generous in youth. But below the topsoil, the chalk maintains structure and finesse — and that is what makes long ageing possible.

Sun from above ripens the fruit. Chalk from below restores the tension. That is the architecture of Bouzy — and the reason Pierre Paillard's wines arrive full but never heavy, oak-aged but never dominated by wood, Pinot Noir at the centre but chalk in the finish. If you want to understand Bouzy for the first time, this is a reliable bottle to start with.

"Bouzy gives you the fruit. The chalk gives you the reason to keep drinking. Pierre Paillard understands both — and knows which one should speak last."

Emmanuel Brochet

Rosé d'Assemblage 2022 — Extra Brut

Champagne · Villers-aux-Nœuds Premier Cru · Cuvée de Négoce · 6,111 bottles

Not a heavier rosé, but a more transparent one — Meunier expressed through assemblage rather than saignée.

Emmanuel Brochet farms 2.5 hectares of a single vineyard, Le Mont Benoît, in Villers-aux-Nœuds Premier Cru, southwest of Reims. He took over part of the family vines in 1997 and made his first bottling in 2002. The southeast-facing slope sits on a clay-silt topsoil above a chalk bedrock; the vineyard holds Ecocert organic certification. His existing wines are pure expressions of Le Mont Benoît — and that is precisely what makes this rosé different.

A Cuvée de Négoce. The Rosé d'Assemblage is the exception in Brochet's lineup — it uses purchased fruit, making it a micro-négoce project rather than a domaine wine. The core Meunier comes from organically farmed old vines planted in 1965 in the surrounding area. Brochet takes delivery of the harvested grapes, presses them himself, and vinifies from that point. It is neighbouring fruit, interpreted through his methods.

Vinification. A 2,000 kg vertical press, with strict selection of the coeur de cuvée — the central fraction of the first pressing. No chaptalization. Fermentation with indigenous yeasts. The rosé is then aged on lees in oak for eleven months. No fining, no filtration. Dosage at Extra Brut level.

Why Assemblage, not Saignée. Brochet's previous rosé was a Rosé de Saignée — colour and structure drawn from skin maceration. He found the result more structured and gastronomic, but heavier. The shift to Rosé d'Assemblage — adding a red wine component to a white base — was a deliberate pursuit of freshness, transparency and drinkability. The first release in this style received 94 points from Wine Advocate.

In the Glass. Reductive nuance at first, then pomegranate, raspberry, redcurrant and blood orange. Rose petals, white pepper and cherry skin follow. On the palate, the wine is lighter and more precise than the colour suggests. Oak and lees ageing bring a Burgundian texture, but citrus oil, low dosage and chalky salinity pull the finish back into tension. The red fruit intensity is clear throughout — but it never moves toward sweetness. That restraint is the point.

The Domaine's Direction. The broader range is also evolving. Where Brochet once bottled the Chardonnay and Meunier from Le Mont Benoît's upper slope separately — as Les Hauts Chardonnays and Les Hauts Meuniers — he has recently merged them into a single 50:50 blend called Les Hautes Vignes. The logic: the terroir of the upper slope matters more than the distinction between varieties. The rosé follows the same trajectory. The 2023 base shifts to 60% Meunier and 40% Chardonnay, with 10% Meunier red wine and 2022 reserve wine added. Rosé d'Assemblage is not a fixed recipe — it is a continuing search for the most transparent possible expression of Meunier.

Dom Ruinart 2010 is not simply an aged prestige Champagne. The house traces its origins to the Benedictine monk Dom Thierry Ruinart, who recognised the potential of the new sparkling wines gaining favour among Parisian aristocracy. Creamy but firm; richly toasted, yet returning at the last to Chardonnay's acidity and chalk.

Le Privé

French Dining in Gangnam, and the Touch of Chef Kihak Lim — Le Privé at The Privy House

Among the many things that stay with you after a visit to The Privy House, Le Privé — the French dining restaurant on the 37th floor — is the one that lingers longest. The menu follows the classical architecture of French dining, but without the weight or the intimidation that can come with it. Long-cooked onion sweetness, deep umami, the nutty warmth of Emmental and Gruyère. The concentration is present without heaviness. A bowl that sets the direction of a meal without announcing itself.

Poisson Meunière. Fish gently cooked and set in a sauce built on vermouth, fish stock and butter. The argument for meunière, as a technique, is ultimately about how elegantly the butter is handled — whether it amplifies the fish or overwhelms it. Magret de Canard. The surprise of the evening was the duck — and the unexpected highlight was what accompanied it: French fries cooked in duck fat. That was the reason to see The Privy House differently this time. Not a room with a view, but a place to eat real French food with a good bottle. With Chef Kihak Lim's hand on Le Privé, it is worth coming back slowly, and with wine.

Tradition is not something old. It is something still alive — A summer lunch at Michelin-starred Onjium

A summer progression might begin with grains and vegetables, gather weight through fish and seafood, and settle into meat and rice before closing with fruit and desserts that reimagine traditional beverages. Onjium's food neither defamiliarises tradition into strangeness nor fixes it behind glass like a museum object. What I encountered at Onjium in July was less a past version of Korean food than Korean food as it continues to be made — across a long time, and still now.

Sutimun

Charcoal Heat, Palace Time — Chef Yunhaeng Heo's Sutimun and the New Language of Korean Dining

A restaurant's transformation is rarely announced by a change in a few dishes. What truly signals change is the decision about what to place at the centre — and in which direction to extend the restaurant's existing identity. The shift perceptible at Sutimun since Chef Yunhaeng Heo took over the kitchen in February begins exactly there.

Sutimun was originally built around a sequence dining concept centred on charcoal and moon — a progression through vegetables, seafood, poultry and meat, each course composed like a discrete scene, bound together by charcoal cookery, craft objects and spatial design into a single coherent narrative. The visible hearth and the rhythm of service around it were unmistakably Sutimun's own.

Under Chef Heo, Sutimun has not abandoned that identity. Instead it is deepening it — moving in a distinctly more Korean direction. The current episode is titled 고궁진연 古宮珍宴 — Royal Feast, the Resonance of Time: the culinary culture of the old palace, the work of contemporary Korean craft artists, and the produce of regional growers, brought together over charcoal and rendered as a modern course.

Not reproduction, but translation. Court cuisine is often imagined as elaborate presentation and formal display. But its essence lies elsewhere — in the balanced use of meat, vegetable and seafood; in the ordered deployment of diverse techniques (steaming, grilling, hot pot, pan-fried, blanched, raw) within a single meal; in the accumulated craft of dedicated cooks, transmitted across generations. Chef Heo's approach is less the restoration of historical dishes than the translation of court cuisine's underlying order, harmony and precision into the language of today.

At Sutimun, charcoal is not merely a concept. It sears a surface quickly, locking in texture; it concentrates the moisture and sweetness within an ingredient; it adds a low, persistent register of aroma that steaming or simmering cannot. But charcoal also carries a risk — if the smoke is too assertive, it overwhelms delicate ingredients and pulls every dish toward the same flavour. The grammar of court cuisine, which prizes restraint and balance, could easily clash with an emphatic char.

What impressed me most was precisely that Sutimun does not make charcoal the protagonist. In the dishes that worked best, the char was not the first thing you registered — it was the last: a brief trailing note that arrived after the sweetness and umami of the ingredient had already been drawn out. This is cookery that uses fire not to demonstrate fire, but to clarify what the ingredient already is.

Orchestrating the refinement of court cuisine and the raw energy of charcoal within a single course is not straightforward. Rather than forcing the two together, Sutimun positions charcoal as a punctuation mark inside the ordered flow that court cuisine provides. That restraint is the most persuasive evidence of what has changed under Chef Heo.

Sutimun's transformation is interesting because it has not erased the past in the name of novelty. Placing the time of the palace on the heat of charcoal; finding, in the quick rhythms of Yeouido, the long breath that Korean food has always carried. That is the most meaningful thing Chef Yunhaeng Heo's Sutimun is showing us now.

We came for the wine. We left remembering the restaurant. A night that began as a search for somewhere to drink became, in the end, a reminder of how elegantly great food, great service, and one chef's singular world can align. One more reason to return — and to bring another bottle.

Restaurant SAN

From the World's Kitchens, Back to the Taste of Korea

On July 22nd, I sat down to experience the summer menu at Restaurant SAN. At the heart of this Michelin-recognised destination is Chef Seunghyun Cho — a Korean-born cook who spent years refining his culinary language in the world's most demanding kitchens before returning home.

His résumé reads like a pilgrimage through fine dining's inner sanctum: The French Laundry in Napa Valley, Troisgros in Roanne, Piazza Duomo in Italy, and Geranium in Copenhagen. He then made his way to San Francisco, joining Chef Corey Lee at the three-Michelin-starred Benu, where he spent roughly eight years as Chef de Cuisine.

Benu is a restaurant that interprets the flavours of Korea, China, and East Asia through the precision of Western fine dining. Working there, Chef Cho didn't simply acquire technique — he learned how to translate the taste of one culture into the language of another. That foundation of classical French rigour and deep East Asian sensibility is what underpins everything SAN stands for today.

He returned to Korea and opened his first independent restaurant in 2024. SAN is not a showcase for what he learned abroad — it is closer to a personal reckoning: after years away, a careful re-examination of Korean flavour through his own eyes and hands.

Two currents run through the food simultaneously. The first is the precision absorbed from world-class kitchens — the calibration of sauce density and temperature, the contrast of textures, the deliberate arc of flavour within a single plate. The second is the Korea lodged in his memory: jeotgal, dongchimi, milmyeon, dwaeji gukbap. Both are present, and neither overwhelms the other.

The Michelin Guide describes SAN as a restaurant that pairs the intensity of seasonal ingredients with complex sauces while maintaining restrained balance. It singles out the abalone jang, tofu flower, and pork gukbap as signature expressions of Chef Cho's imagination and Korean interpretation. That direction was unmistakable in the summer course.

The meal opens with the coolness and bright acidity of summer produce, then builds gradually — through the umami of seafood, the depth of fermentation, and the warmth of meat and fire — without ever becoming heavy. The sauces are layered but never mask the ingredient. The technique is evident but never intrudes. You sense the years in America and Europe, but every dish ultimately resolves toward Korea.

The space suits the name perfectly. Tucked underground, the restaurant is insulated from the city above. Inside, the architecture echoes forests and ridgelines — tree-ring textures, restrained lighting, dark tones — creating a quiet that allows the food and wine to hold the room.

In 2026, SAN received the Asia's 50 Best Restaurants One To Watch Award — recognition for a restaurant still early in its journey, yet already carrying the weight of everything Chef Cho accumulated across the world's great kitchens.

A chef who spent years in the world's finest kitchens, finally returning to the taste he was always moving toward. The summer at Restaurant SAN felt like both a record of the distance he has travelled and a clear signal of where he intends to go.
