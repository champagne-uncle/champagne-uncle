Krug

Clos du Mesnil 2009 — Blanc de Blancs Brut

Champagne · Le Mesnil-sur-Oger Grand Cru · 1.84 ha · Edition 21

A house built on the art of assemblage — and its most singular argument against it.

When you think of Krug, the first word is assemblage. Every year, hundreds of individual wines are tasted, parcels and vintages combined, to complete Grande Cuvée — this is the philosophical centre of the house. Which makes Clos du Mesnil, one of Krug's rarest Champagnes, a direct contradiction: one vineyard, one variety, one harvest.

1.84 hectares of walled vines at the heart of Le Mesnil-sur-Oger. 100% Chardonnay. No blending of parcels or vintages. If any house understood the case for assemblage, it was Krug — and if Krug decided a single vineyard was worth a standalone wine, the vineyard had to be extraordinary.

The story begins in 1971, when Krug purchased the Clos to secure a reliable source of high-quality Chardonnay for Grande Cuvée. During the separate vinification of each parcel, the Clos wine consistently stood apart — unusually pure, unusually clear. The exceptional 1979 harvest was bottled separately, released in 1986, and Clos du Mesnil was born. The 2009 is the 21st edition.

Inside the Wall. Even within 1.84 hectares, cellar master Julie Cavil identifies six micro-plots. Older vines cast morning shadow on younger neighbours. Stone walls reflect heat unevenly. Planting dates vary. Ripening is staggered. The Clos is not a single block — it is a small world.

The Vintage. 2009 began with a hard winter. Spring was mild; budburst arrived mid-April. May through July brought repeated storms. Then the season changed entirely: a dry, sunny summer, with warm days and cool nights through harvest. Krug describes 2009 as a year of abundant, delicate fruit with strength and volume. Harvest at the Clos: 10–11 September. Not the austere, long-ageing structure of 2008 — the Chardonnay was riper, the fruit more layered. And yet Le Mesnil's chalk and the Clos's particular microclimate preserved strong acidity and salinity within the ripe fruit. Production: 9,900 bottles — down from the usual 12,000.

In the Glass. Honeysuckle, white flowers and a faint vegetal lift open the nose; gingerbread, brioche and orange marmalade follow, with multiple citrus registers beneath. On the palate, ripe lemon, mandarin pastry and a sweet pear note are present, alongside a pleasant saltiness, nuts and light spice. Concentrated but delicate; structurally firm. The chalk of Le Mesnil is fully present. More time is needed — this is not yet at its centre of gravity.

"Krug built its name by combining everything. Clos du Mesnil is the one moment it steps back and lets a single place speak for itself. Le Mesnil does not disappoint — but it is not ready to say everything yet."

— Champagne Uncle

Dom Ruinart

Blanc de Blancs Extra Brut 2010

Champagne · Ruinart · Reims · Est. 1729

The oldest Champagne house, reviving a forgotten method to make a vintage for the future.

Dom Ruinart 2010 is not simply an aged prestige Champagne. It is closer to a winemaking inflection point — the moment when Ruinart, the world's oldest Champagne house (founded 1729), revived a long-dormant tradition and reset the direction of Dom Ruinart. The house traces its origins to the Benedictine monk Dom Thierry Ruinart, who recognised the potential of the new sparkling wines gaining favour among Parisian aristocracy. His nephew Nicolas Ruinart turned that vision into the first Champagne company, established in Reims in 1729.

Dom Ruinart as a cuvée was first produced in 1959 and released in 1966 — a homage to Dom Thierry himself. Through 2010, only 27 vintages have been declared.

The Blend. 100% Grand Cru Chardonnay: 90% from Le Mesnil-sur-Oger, Avize, Chouilly and Cramant; 10% from Sillery on the Montagne de Reims.

Why toasted almond and coffee — without oak. Fermentation in stainless steel; malolactic completed; no barrels. And yet the finished wine shows roasted almond, hazelnut, coffee and toast clearly. The answer is tirage liège — natural cork for secondary fermentation and ageing, instead of metal crown cap. 2010 was the first modern vintage in which Ruinart fully returned to natural cork ageing for Dom Ruinart since the 1960s. Micro-oxygenation through cork preserves freshness while developing umami depth and aromatic complexity. Disgorgement returned to hand-work. Minimum nine years on lees; roughly twelve years from harvest to release. Dosage: 4 g/l.

In the Glass. The nose opens on powdery white flowers, iris and wet-stone mineral. With time: nutmeg, roasted hazelnut, almond, coffee. On the palate, fig leaf, black tea and white pepper; the finish carries bitter citrus peel, salinity and chalk. Creamy but firm; richly toasted, yet returning at the last to Chardonnay's acidity and chalk.

"Dom Ruinart 2010 is an argument made in cork and chalk — that patience, and a decision to do things the old way, can produce something a shortcut never could."

— Champagne Uncle

Krug

Vintage Brut 1998 — Hommage au Chardonnay

Champagne · Reims · Grande Maison · 27 years on

Twenty-seven years later, Krug 1998 has not finished. But this bottle had already moved further.

In the summer of 1998, Champagne baked under an August heatwave. Pinot Noir and Meunier absorbed that heat and ripened into something generous and sensual. But when cellar master Julie Cavil tasted the early samples, she made a different decision — she would lead with Chardonnay. Krug called this vintage Hommage au Chardonnay: one of the very few times since 1981 that Chardonnay was given primacy in the blend.

The Blend. Chardonnay 46%, Pinot Noir 35%, Meunier 19%. Dosage: 6 g/l. Released 2012 after thirteen years in Krug's cellars. Wine Spectator awarded 98 points: "This harmonious Champagne shows a sumptuous texture and concentrated acidity." Most experts placed the drinking window through 2030.

This Bottle. A standard Krug at twenty-five to thirty years tends to show fresh-baked bread, hazelnut, honey, dried fruit and caramelised orange — followed by citrus pastry, ginger, and the first whisper of mushroom. This bottle had moved past that stage. Earth and mushroom were leading; Scotch candy and roasted fruit followed. The acidity, while present, did not arrive with the firmness or intensity one expects at this age. In its place: layered complexity, umami, and a pleasant salinity. Reviews on CellarTracker describe vibrant acidity and bright fruit on some bottles, smoky tertiary notes on others. Bottle condition clearly governs the experience.

Slightly short of what I had hoped for — but a full experience nonetheless. Reason enough to find another 1998. If the opportunity arises, a 1995 or a fresh bottle of the same vintage would be worth pursuing.

"The 1998 chose Chardonnay in a Pinot year. This bottle chose to move on without waiting for permission. Slightly ahead of where I wanted it — but Krug at any stage remains a conversation worth having."

— Champagne Uncle

Pierre Paillard

'La Grande Récolte' 2008 — Bouzy Grand Cru Extra Brut

Champagne · Bouzy · Montagne de Reims

Fifteen years of ageing — where Bouzy's sun and the acidity of 2008 finally come together.

La Grande Récolte is not Pierre Paillard's non-vintage cuvée. It is the domaine's benchmark vintage Champagne — made only in exceptional years, from the oldest vines on the estate. The blend is Pinot Noir 55% and Chardonnay 45%: the Pinot from Les Maillerettes (planted 1970); the Chardonnay from Les Mottelettes (planted 1961). Both parcels are today bottled as individual single-vineyard cuvées, but in the 2008 La Grande Récolte they appear together — a record of how these two historic old-vine sites expressed themselves before they were separated.

The Vintage. 2008 in Champagne was a cool, overcast growing season, but a dry and sunny September allowed the grapes to ripen slowly and completely. The result: high natural acidity, concentrated fruit, pronounced salinity, and a long finish — one of modern Champagne's greatest long-ageing vintages.

Vinification & Ageing. Vinified and aged in oak, then left on lees in bottle for approximately fifteen years. Disgorged in 2023. Dosage: 1.8 g/l. The extended lees contact has built sufficient texture and breadth that the wine never feels excessively dry. Production: approximately 833 cases. Retail allocation has largely disappeared.

In the Glass. The nose opens on ripe pear, red apple and yellow fruit, followed by lemon curd, orange peel, quince and redcurrant. With time: brioche, almond, dried fruit and honey. On the palate, the broad texture of Bouzy Pinot Noir arrives first, but the high acidity of 2008 and the Chardonnay pull the wine long and precise. The finish carries bitter citrus peel, the dry touch of chalk and a strong, clean salinity. Drink now for the contrast between fruit and tension; wait until the 2030s for dried citrus, hazelnut, honey and toast.

"La Grande Récolte 2008 is what happens when you have the discipline not to rush. The old vines gave the material; the vintage gave the structure; fifteen years gave the depth. None of it could be substituted."

— Champagne Uncle

Tasting Note · October 2026

Chartogne-Taillet

Heurtebise 2022 — Blanc de Blancs Extra Brut

Champagne · Merfy · Montagne de Reims

Chardonnay speaking not in chalk but in sand and sandstone — the Merfy terroir in its most complete expression.

The 2022 vintage was disgorged in May 2026 and released this year. Among the single-parcel cuvées from Chartogne-Taillet, this is the one I return to most readily — for its reductive character, which I find genuinely compelling rather than merely interesting. I found it at Deukine House in Gangseo, and bought it immediately on sight.

Chartogne-Taillet is based in Merfy, northwest of Reims. The Taillet family's viticultural records reach back to the fifteenth century; the label marks 1683 as the beginning of their continuous presence in Merfy. When Alexandre Chartogne took over the domaine in 2006, he began moving away from house-style blending toward wines that articulate the geological differences between individual parcels.

The Vineyard. Les Heurtes Bises — the hard winds. South-facing plateau in Merfy, strongly wind-exposed. Soil: approximately 58% sand, 22% silt, 16% clay, on Thanetian calcareous sand, with a firm sandstone layer at ~80cm depth. Because sandstone is less permeable than chalk, water management is active: in wet years the canopy is raised to encourage transpiration; in dry years leaf area is reduced.

Vinification. 100% Chardonnay, indigenous yeasts, aged in 228-litre Burgundy barrels for nine to eighteen months. Malolactic fermentation proceeds naturally; no fining, no filtration. The barrel is a textural tool — not for flavour, but to wrap the parcel's tension and sharp sandstone minerality in something softer. 2022 dosage: approximately 2.4 g/l Extra Brut.

In the Glass. Toasted grain, pear, honey-poached apricot, lemon zest and white flowers open the nose; dried flowers, chamomile and quiet spice emerge with time. On the palate, the mousse is very fine and the texture approaches cream, but the wine's centre of gravity is tension rather than fruit weight. Mid-palate salinity is distinct; the finish carries lemon peel and the faintest impression of sandstone.

"Most Champagne asks you to enjoy it. Heurtebise asks you to pay attention. The sandstone speaks quietly, but once you hear it, you notice it doesn't stop."

— Champagne Uncle

Louis Roederer
Cristal Rosé 2008

Champagne · Reims · Montagne de Reims

There are vintages and there are generations. 2008 is the latter — a year so structurally precise and so charged with energy that it continues to redefine what Champagne can be. Among all the great wines of that harvest, Cristal Rosé occupies a singular position: the most intimate expression of Louis Roederer's century-long conversation with a single estate.

Cristal has been produced since 1876, originally at the request of Tsar Alexander II — who wanted his wine in a clear crystal bottle, so he could see it had not been tampered with. The Rosé is rarer still: a saignée of Pinot Noir from the domaine's own vineyards in Cumières and Aÿ, blended with Chardonnay for length, aged for a decade before release. It is not a wine made for the market. It is a wine made for the cellar.

The 2008 arrives in the glass with the composure of something that has waited patiently and knows it was right to. The nose is precise and layered — wild strawberry, blood orange, dried rose petals, white pepper, and beneath it all a mineral chalk that pulls everything into focus. On the palate, the acidity is the revelation: not aggressive, but structural, holding the fruit in suspension like a frame holds a painting.

Wine Spectator's Alison Napjus wrote: "Like fine china, this graceful rosé Champagne is all about delicacy paired with form. Vibrant acidity carries flavours of cherry, blood orange and wild strawberry, all framed by finely woven tannins." 95 points. Wine-Searcher consensus: 97.

October is the month the harvest ends and the silence begins — when winemakers finally stand back and let time do what only time can. Cristal Rosé 2008 is a wine about exactly that patience. Open it now with something worth the occasion, or hold it another decade and see what becomes of it.

"Cristal Rosé isn't Champagne trying to be something else. It's Champagne at its most certain of itself — Pinot Noir that knows exactly where it came from, aged long enough to stop proving it."

— Champagne Uncle

Larmandier-Bernier
Les Chemins d'Avize

Champagne · Grower & Producer · Avize, Côte des Blancs

Few addresses in grower Champagne carry the moral authority of Larmandier-Bernier. Pierre and Sophie Larmandier have farmed biodynamically since the early 2000s, pressing their own fruit, fermenting with indigenous yeasts, and ageing in Stockinger casks — a discipline that has made Vertus and Avize speak more clearly than almost anywhere else on the Côte des Blancs.

Les Chemins d'Avize is the latest and most singular of their single-parcel cuvées. It draws from two vieilles vignes plots — Chemin de Plivot and Chemin de Flavigny — where chalk outcrops at the surface and 65-year-old vines yield with extreme parsimony. The juice goes directly into wood, ferments spontaneously, and rests on lees for a full year before spending at least nine more years on lattes in the cellar. Dosage: 2 g/l. Each bottle disgorged by hand, one year before release.

What arrives in the glass is not a wine that announces itself. The nose is quiet and precise — white flowers, lemongrass, verbena, chalk dust, and a low register of stone fruit held in cool restraint. On the palate, the texture is silky but the mineral density is emphatic: the chalk of Avize rendered as energy rather than weight, finishing with the clean cut of a wine that still has years ahead of it.

Essi Avellan MW called it "quietly spoken elegance — the silky texture and mineral bite bring notions of energy and finesse." Robert Parker awarded the 2014 vintage 96 points; Vinous followed at 95. Wine-Searcher aggregates 45 critic reviews at a consensus of 94 points.

September is the month the harvest begins in Champagne. There is no better moment to open a wine that took a decade to reach the table — and to understand, through one glass, why patience and chalk are the only two things Avize has ever needed.

"Chardonnay as the purest argument. The oak is everywhere and nowhere — it builds the architecture, then steps aside. Larmandier-Bernier uses wood the way great Burgundy does: not to flavour, but to breathe."

— Champagne Uncle

Mousse Fils
Hommage à Cuisles

Champagne · Grower & Producer · Cuisles, Vallée de la Marne

Cédric Mousse is one of the most compelling voices in grower Champagne today. His Hommage à Cuisles is a tribute to the village where his family has farmed for generations — a single-village expression built on Pinot Meunier, vinified without malolactic fermentation to preserve tension and clarity. The nose offers white flowers, ripe pear, brioche and a subtle touch of spice. On the palate, the texture is silky yet precise, with bright citrus acidity, crushed chalk minerality and a long, saline finish. Extra Brut dosage keeps the wine taut and expressive — a portrait of terroir rather than house style.

"Mousse Fils remains one of the benchmark addresses for Meunier-driven Champagne — pure, purposeful and deeply rooted in place." — Champagne Uncle

Henri Giraud
Fût de Chêne MV20

Champagne Grand Cru · Aÿ · Pinot Noir dominant

Henri Giraud's Fût de Chêne MV20 is one of Champagne's most distinctive and deliberately crafted expressions. Aged in small Argonne oak barrels — a forest that Giraud sources and selects himself — the wine undergoes full malolactic fermentation in wood, building extraordinary texture and depth. The MV (Multi-Vintage) blend draws on reserve wines from Aÿ's grand cru vineyards, anchored by Pinot Noir with contributions of Chardonnay and Meunier. The nose is complex and warming: toasted brioche, roasted hazelnut, dried apricot, and a signature cedar-oak spice. On the palate, the mousse is creamy and persistent, the acidity perfectly integrated, and the finish long with notes of salted caramel and forest floor. A wine that rewards patience — and demands attention.

"The barrel work is seamless — you taste the oak as architecture, not ornament. This is Champagne with the soul of Burgundy." — Champagne Uncle

Le Privé

French Dining in Gangnam, and the Touch of Chef Kihak Lim — Le Privé at The Privy House

Among the many things that stay with you after a visit to The Privy House, Le Privé — the French dining restaurant on the 37th floor — is the one that lingers longest. The view is extraordinary, the atmosphere composed. But what the meal itself makes clear is that this is not simply a beautiful room with a restaurant inside it.

What made it immediately interesting was the involvement of Chef Kihak Lim as consultant. The menu follows the classical architecture of French dining, but without the weight or the intimidation that can come with it. Familiar French cooking rendered in a present-day register — the fundamentals of sauce and technique intact, but worn lightly. You sense, eating here, that the food has its own centre of gravity.

Soupe à l'Oignon. Onion soup is one of those dishes whose quality is impossible to hide — it is too familiar, and its grammar too well known. Long-cooked onion sweetness, deep umami, the nutty warmth of Emmental and Gruyère. The concentration is present without heaviness. A bowl that sets the direction of a meal without announcing itself.

Poisson Meunière. Fish gently cooked and set in a sauce built on vermouth, fish stock and butter. The argument for meunière, as a technique, is ultimately about how elegantly the butter is handled — whether it amplifies the fish or overwhelms it. Here the butter is distinct and present, and the fish remains the subject.

Magret de Canard. The surprise of the evening was the duck — and the unexpected highlight was what accompanied it: French fries cooked in duck fat. Something so simple, done in duck fat, becomes something else entirely. The surface is crisp, the potato flavour clean, and behind it a low register of duck that stays. It was as memorable as the main itself. Duck as a protein rewards proper cooking with a depth that pulls you toward Burgundy Pinot Noir, an aged Bordeaux, or a more structured Champagne.

French dining's pleasure lies here: you need not reach for rare or complex ingredients. What changes the depth of a dish is how the butter, stock, sauce and fat are handled. Le Privé's menu — soupe à l'oignon, salade lyonnaise, beef tartare, duck croquette, meunière, bouillabaisse, braised pork, magret, steak — is familiar from a hundred French bistros and brasseries. What is not familiar is the precision with which it has been assembled for Seoul, and for now.

If The Privy House draws you with its view and its privacy, Le Privé is what makes you stay. Good dining is not completed by interior design alone — it requires people, food, and wine, held together by a chef's experience and instinct. That was the reason to see The Privy House differently this time. Not a room with a view, but a place to eat real French food with a good bottle. With Chef Kihak Lim's hand on Le Privé, it is worth coming back slowly, and with wine.

Onjium

Tradition is not something old. It is something still alive — A summer lunch at Michelin-starred Onjium

On the 8th of July, I visited Onjium, a Michelin one-star restaurant situated to the west of Gyeongbokgung Palace. To call it simply a Korean fine dining restaurant would be to leave too much unsaid. Onjium is first a traditional culture research institute, founded in 2013, which investigates Korean ways of dressing, eating and living — ui, sik, ju — and attempts to re-articulate them in a contemporary language. The restaurant is where the research of the food atelier, mat-gongbang, is realised as actual dishes. True to its name — which carries the sense of building something properly and wholly — every plate is accompanied by inquiry and interpretation.

The kitchen is led by Head Chef Eunhee Cho and Executive Chef Seongbae Park. Chef Cho is a certified inheritor of Joseon royal court cuisine, having studied at the Royal Cuisine Research Institute before serving as education team leader and adjunct university professor; she was among the founding members of Onjium in 2013, and in 2026 was named Asia's Best Female Chef. Chef Park bridges the atelier's research into the lived experience of a restaurant meal. Together, rather than restoring historical recipes wholesale, they recompose them in relation to today's ingredients and sensibility.

The season is legible in Onjium's courses. A summer progression might begin with grains and vegetables, gather weight through fish and seafood, and settle into meat and rice before closing with fruit and desserts that reimagine traditional beverages. The forms are recognisable — porridge, dumpling, jeon, jjim, noodle, banchan — but the concentration, temperature and texture of each dish have been quietly and precisely calibrated.

Seasonal markers such as corn, local fish, crustaceans, white cucumber and cornelian cherry appear when summer calls for them. Because Onjium's menu shifts continuously with research themes and ingredient availability, the more rewarding way to approach the meal is not to track individual dishes but to observe how a single season moves through the arc of an entire course.

The room speaks as clearly as the food. Exposed concrete, metal and warm timber come together in a space that is modern without coldness. White tables, composed tableware and ceramics bearing the evidence of a craftsperson's hand rest in considered negative space. At the counter facing the open kitchen, the choreography of the kitchen is visible and close.

Through wide windows, Gyeongbokgung Palace, the trees of Seochon and the ridge of Bugaksan extend outward. In the middle of the day, natural light travels deep into the room and changes the quality of glaze and surface on every piece of pottery. To look down at a palace while eating food that departs from centuries-old techniques: it is here that Onjium's space and its cooking make their most persuasive argument together.

Onjium's food neither defamiliarises tradition into strangeness nor fixes it behind glass like a museum object. It honours old records and techniques while drawing them quietly forward to the point where we might find them delicious, today. What I encountered at Onjium in July was less a past version of Korean food than Korean food as it continues to be made — across a long time, and still now.

Sutimun

Charcoal Heat, Palace Time — Chef Yunhaeng Heo's Sutimun and the New Language of Korean Dining

A restaurant's transformation is rarely announced by a change in a few dishes. What truly signals change is the decision about what to place at the centre — and in which direction to extend the restaurant's existing identity. The shift perceptible at Sutimun since Chef Yunhaeng Heo took over the kitchen in February begins exactly there.

Sutimun was originally built around a sequence dining concept centred on charcoal and moon — a progression through vegetables, seafood, poultry and meat, each course composed like a discrete scene, bound together by charcoal cookery, craft objects and spatial design into a single coherent narrative. The visible hearth and the rhythm of service around it were unmistakably Sutimun's own.

Under Chef Heo, Sutimun has not abandoned that identity. Instead it is deepening it — moving in a distinctly more Korean direction. The current episode is titled 고궁진연 古宮珍宴 — Royal Feast, the Resonance of Time: the culinary culture of the old palace, the work of contemporary Korean craft artists, and the produce of regional growers, brought together over charcoal and rendered as a modern course.

Not reproduction, but translation. Court cuisine is often imagined as elaborate presentation and formal display. But its essence lies elsewhere — in the balanced use of meat, vegetable and seafood; in the ordered deployment of diverse techniques (steaming, grilling, hot pot, pan-fried, blanched, raw) within a single meal; in the accumulated craft of dedicated cooks, transmitted across generations. Chef Heo's approach is less the restoration of historical dishes than the translation of court cuisine's underlying order, harmony and precision into the language of today.

At Sutimun, charcoal is not merely a concept. It sears a surface quickly, locking in texture; it concentrates the moisture and sweetness within an ingredient; it adds a low, persistent register of aroma that steaming or simmering cannot. But charcoal also carries a risk — if the smoke is too assertive, it overwhelms delicate ingredients and pulls every dish toward the same flavour. The grammar of court cuisine, which prizes restraint and balance, could easily clash with an emphatic char.

What impressed me most was precisely that Sutimun does not make charcoal the protagonist. In the dishes that worked best, the char was not the first thing you registered — it was the last: a brief trailing note that arrived after the sweetness and umami of the ingredient had already been drawn out. This is cookery that uses fire not to demonstrate fire, but to clarify what the ingredient already is.

Orchestrating the refinement of court cuisine and the raw energy of charcoal within a single course is not straightforward. Rather than forcing the two together, Sutimun positions charcoal as a punctuation mark inside the ordered flow that court cuisine provides. That restraint is the most persuasive evidence of what has changed under Chef Heo.

Sutimun's transformation is interesting because it has not erased the past in the name of novelty. Placing the time of the palace on the heat of charcoal; finding, in the quick rhythms of Yeouido, the long breath that Korean food has always carried. That is the most meaningful thing Chef Yunhaeng Heo's Sutimun is showing us now.

Hashira

The Celebration Begins at the Counter — Chef Joohwan Yoon's Rhythm and Craft

Four of us visited Hashira, a sushi-ya in Cheongdam. When people think of sushi omakase, a particular kind of quiet tension usually comes to mind — the lowered voices, the eyes following the chef's hands, the brief pause of breath as each piece is set down. That concentration, particular to Edomae sushi, is absolutely present at Hashira.

But Chef Joohwan Yoon's counter is not completed by tension alone.

The four of us exhale together at a single piece, glasses make their rounds, the temperature of the conversation rises. A natural exchange opens up between chef and guests. By the midpoint of the meal, the counter has quietly transformed — no longer a solemn stage, but a small celebration in which everyone is a participant. Chef Yoon stands at its centre.

The shari at Hashira has a presence that insists on being noticed. Rather than retreating quietly behind the neta, it uses acidity and salinity to draw out the flavour of the fish — cleansing the palate on richer, fattier pieces. In leaner fish, sweetness and delicate texture come forward; in oiled neta, the flavour doesn't linger heavily. It is precisely why Hashira is described as a place where the character of the shari is clear, and sake pairings feel entirely natural.

The nigiri leaves its mark not through size or flourish, but through balance and timing. The thickness of the neta, the score of the knife, the weight and temperature of the shari — each is calibrated so that fish and rice move as one. The moment it enters the mouth, the texture of the neta arrives first; the shari follows, dissolving softly as acidity and umami extend into a single, continuous finish. Rather than making any one piece overwhelming, the course is designed to carry you naturally toward the next — a sushi built around the arc of the whole.

But the reason Hashira feels like a celebration is not simply that the food and sake are generous. Chef Yoon is as skilled at reading the breath of the entire counter as he is at forming each piece. He watches the pace at which guests eat, the moment a glass is lifted, the point at which conversation among the four of us reaches its warmth — and places the next dish accordingly. When concentration is called for, he draws every eye at the counter toward his hands. When tension runs high, a natural word or two from him dissolves it.

That evening at Hashira, the four of us fell quiet together over a single piece of sushi — and then laughed again, almost at once. The precision of Edomae technique and the warmth of genuine hospitality found each other naturally within the same counter. The night the four of us shared became something that outlasted any single piece of sushi — a memory a little larger than the meal itself.

Sosuheon

The Art of Balance — Chef Kyungjae Park's Gastronomy

What I felt most acutely at Sosuheon — the recently crowned Michelin two-star — was the philosophy that Chef Kyungjae Park has spent a lifetime building. Over more than thirty years devoted entirely to sushi, through the Shilla Hotel and Kojima before opening his own space, he led Korea's first Michelin two-star sushi at Kojima and received the Michelin Mentor Chef Award in 2023. Sosuheon's own ascent to two stars this year is confirmation that his philosophy remains as vital as ever.

That impression arrived with the very first tsumami. Each seasonal ingredient was read with precision — umami present but never excessive, salinity calibrated to awaken the palate without overwhelming it, acidity functioning like negative space between courses. Nothing jutted forward; everything supported everything else. What struck me was not the ability to add flavour, but the ability to edit it.

Moving into the nigiri, that delicacy became even more pronounced. The neta was captured at its precise peak. The shari — warm, correctly salted, dissolving with a gentle give — existed not to assert itself but to make the fish more vivid. The technique Chef Park has refined over decades manifests not as spectacle but as a balance so natural the diner barely registers it. Michelin describes Sosuheon as a place where "the refined touch of a master who quietly focuses on forming each nigiri" defines the experience — and sitting before the counter, that description felt exactly right.

One particularly memorable moment was the lobster with truffle. It is a combination where truffle can easily colonise every other flavour, but here it did not. The sweetness and yielding texture of the lobster remained at the centre; the truffle was given the role of layering fragrance, nothing more. Luxurious ingredients were deployed, yet the ingredient itself remained the subject. The ability to allow each element its own character while resolving them into a single coherent flavour — this was perhaps the purest expression of Chef Park's gastronomic thinking on a single plate.

What lingered longest after the meal was the word restraint. Many restaurants reach for intensity to produce surprise. Sosuheon never loses its balance. Umami, salinity, acidity, temperature, aroma, texture, the arc of the course — every element is fitted together with the precision of joinery, continuing like a single unbroken narrative. That balance is harder to achieve than spectacle: Chef Park demonstrates this quietly, and overwhelmingly.

A Michelin two-star does not mean a single outstanding dish. It means a restaurant that sustains a high level of accomplishment across an entire course while communicating a chef's philosophy with consistency. Sosuheon made that standard feel entirely self-evident.

In the end, what was most delicious at Sosuheon was not any single dish. It was the balance that Chef Kyungjae Park never once let go of — from the first bite to the last.

Mingles

Finding a Place to Drink Wine — A Night at Mingles

Finding a place to drink wine isn't simply a matter of choosing somewhere to open a bottle. It means finding a place where the food is good, where it doesn't get in the way of the wine — and where, sometimes, it draws out the wine's full potential. On this particular night, that place was Mingles.

We brought one bottle ourselves; the rest we ordered from the menu, exploring as the evening unfolded. And those choices came naturally. As glasses changed, plates arrived, and the pace of conversation gradually slowed, the food at Mingles made its presence known — clearly, without exaggeration.

What struck me most was the harmony. Multiple layers were unmistakably present, yet none pushed forward to disrupt the whole. The flavours ran deep, the textures were delicate, and the aromas accumulated in quiet layers. Complex without being difficult, refined without being excessive. It is rare to find a dining experience where the word "balance" rises so naturally to mind.

Another pleasure was the unrepetitive rhythm. Rather than demonstrating consistent mastery through a single approach, the intention seemed to be giving guests as wide a range of flavour and texture as possible. Soft and sharp, warm and cool, the familiar and the unexpected — each plate arrived in a different register, so the meal felt less like a conventional course and more like a well-edited sequence of scenes.

What made it especially memorable were the dishes that felt genuinely new to the dining scene — flavours I hadn't encountered before, yet rendered with a conviction that made them immediately persuasive. Behind that lay the passion and curiosity of Chef Mingoo Kang. He starts from a foundation of familiar Korean sensibility, then pushes it one step further, translating it into a language that is distinctly Mingles.

The service moved like water. Present when needed, receding when not. The hospitality was genuinely warm — the temperature of the glass, the atmosphere of the table, the intervals between courses all stitched together seamlessly. There was a comfortable tension that only the finest restaurants seem capable of producing.

We came for the wine. We left remembering the restaurant. A night that began as a search for somewhere to drink became, in the end, a reminder of how elegantly great food, great service, and one chef's singular world can align. One more reason to return — and to bring another bottle.

Restaurant SAN

From the World's Kitchens, Back to the Taste of Korea

On July 22nd, I sat down to experience the summer menu at Restaurant SAN. At the heart of this Michelin-recognised destination is Chef Seunghyun Cho — a Korean-born cook who spent years refining his culinary language in the world's most demanding kitchens before returning home.

His résumé reads like a pilgrimage through fine dining's inner sanctum: The French Laundry in Napa Valley, Troisgros in Roanne, Piazza Duomo in Italy, and Geranium in Copenhagen. He then made his way to San Francisco, joining Chef Corey Lee at the three-Michelin-starred Benu, where he spent roughly eight years as Chef de Cuisine.

Benu is a restaurant that interprets the flavours of Korea, China, and East Asia through the precision of Western fine dining. Working there, Chef Cho didn't simply acquire technique — he learned how to translate the taste of one culture into the language of another. That foundation of classical French rigour and deep East Asian sensibility is what underpins everything SAN stands for today.

He returned to Korea and opened his first independent restaurant in 2024. SAN is not a showcase for what he learned abroad — it is closer to a personal reckoning: after years away, a careful re-examination of Korean flavour through his own eyes and hands.

Two currents run through the food simultaneously. The first is the precision absorbed from world-class kitchens — the calibration of sauce density and temperature, the contrast of textures, the deliberate arc of flavour within a single plate. The second is the Korea lodged in his memory: jeotgal, dongchimi, milmyeon, dwaeji gukbap. Both are present, and neither overwhelms the other.

The Michelin Guide describes SAN as a restaurant that pairs the intensity of seasonal ingredients with complex sauces while maintaining restrained balance. It singles out the abalone jang, tofu flower, and pork gukbap as signature expressions of Chef Cho's imagination and Korean interpretation. That direction was unmistakable in the summer course.

The meal opens with the coolness and bright acidity of summer produce, then builds gradually — through the umami of seafood, the depth of fermentation, and the warmth of meat and fire — without ever becoming heavy. The sauces are layered but never mask the ingredient. The technique is evident but never intrudes. You sense the years in America and Europe, but every dish ultimately resolves toward Korea.

The space suits the name perfectly. Tucked underground, the restaurant is insulated from the city above. Inside, the architecture echoes forests and ridgelines — tree-ring textures, restrained lighting, dark tones — creating a quiet that allows the food and wine to hold the room.

In 2026, SAN received the Asia's 50 Best Restaurants One To Watch Award — recognition for a restaurant still early in its journey, yet already carrying the weight of everything Chef Cho accumulated across the world's great kitchens.

A chef who spent years in the world's finest kitchens, finally returning to the taste he was always moving toward. The summer at Restaurant SAN felt like both a record of the distance he has travelled and a clear signal of where he intends to go.
